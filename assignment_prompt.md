## What You're Doing

In Parts 1 and 2 of this assignment, you identified a figure from a paper and explained what method it illustrates and what you want to learn. Now you're doing the learning.

The goal is not to produce a perfect analysis. The goal is to genuinely engage with the method — understand how it works, try to use it or work through it yourself, and reflect on what you understand and what still confuses you. Students have very different backgrounds; what "working through the method" looks like will be different for everyone.

---

## Choose Your Format

**You may submit in either format.** Pick whichever fits how you actually work.

### Format A — The Explainer
A structured document that could serve as a tutorial or reference. Organized by topic (not by date). Should include:
- A brief description of the figure and what it claims
- An explanation of the method (the math, the biology, the experimental logic — whatever is relevant for your figure)
- Evidence that you *tried* the method yourself (code, derivations, sketches, protocol walk-throughs)
- A biological or scientific interpretation: do you believe what the figure claims?
- A reflection (what you learned, what confused you, what you'd do next)
- A worklog section (a table or list of dated entries — doesn't need to be long)

### Format B — The Lab Notebook
A chronological document of your working process. Each entry is dated and describes what you did, what you tried, what you figured out, and what confused you. The worklog *is* the document. Should include:
- A brief framing section at the top (figure, paper, what you're trying to learn)
- Dated entries throughout the work period — include dead ends, corrections, and questions
- At least one entry that explicitly addresses: *do you believe what the figure claims?*
- A final entry with your reflection

---

## What to Include

Include whatever is relevant for your figure and your work:
- **Code** (Jupyter notebook, R script, Python script, etc.)
- **Sketches** of math, diagrams, or experimental setups (photos of hand-drawn work are completely fine)
- **Notes** from papers, videos, or textbooks you used
- **Data** if you used or downloaded a dataset (or a description of the dataset and where to find it)

---

## What This Is Not

This is not a term paper. You do not need:
- An introduction/background section written for a general audience
- A literature review
- Perfect prose or polished figures
- A complete or correct analysis

A notebook that documents a genuine attempt that didn't fully work, with an honest explanation of what went wrong and what you'd try next, is an excellent submission.
